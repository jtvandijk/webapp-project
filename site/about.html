About GBNames

About GBNames

GBNames allows users to explore the British geography of their family, using the search box above to enter a family name. Many family groups first settled in different parts of Great Britain and typically spread out over time. Uneven geographies mean that family groups may be concentrated in different types of neighbourhoods. This site uses surnames to introduce users to the SmartCensus, developed by the Economic and Social Research Council's Geographic Data Service (GeoDS).

This page explains, in plain terms, where the data comes from, how the map is built, and what each of the statistics on a name's page actually shows - with links to the underlying research for anyone who wants the full detail.

Where the data comes from

For the Historic Census period (1851-1921), we use digitised historical census records for England, Wales and Scotland from the Integrated Census Microdata (I-CeM) project, who integrate the decennial censuses under secure data-user agreements with researchers. Addresses are linked to the historic parish they fall in; see Higgs and Schürer (2014) for how this was built. Census data for a given year only becomes available for research once it is 100 years old, which is why 1921 is currently the most recent census year we can use, and why Scotland is missing from the 1911 census (not released on the same schedule as England and Wales).

The SmartCensus is an ongoing project of the Geographic Data Service (GeoDS) to link conventional censuses and surveys with new and exciting 'smart' data sources - providing neighbourhood estimates of circumstances including health, resilience to cost of living pressures and gambling behaviour - in greater geographic detail and with greater update frequency than more conventional statistical sources. Smart data are typically created when we use smartphones and other digital devices to carry out everyday tasks. These data are inherently less reliable than surveys and statistics, so our data scientists must document their quality and provenance; these updates are typically developed for the same neighbourhood areas that are used for the UK Census. We document the quality of our smart and other data on our website (data.geods.ac.uk) and in research publications, including Lansley et al. (2019) and Van Dijk et al. (2021) for the technical detail behind the SmartData periods shown here. We have been updating many of our SmartCensus datasets every year since 1997, allowing researchers to monitor the intricacies of local changes throughout the 21st century. The most recent year, labelled SmartCensus, is the newest wave of this data and the namesake of the whole programme.

How we map your name

Rather than plotting every single address (which would risk identifying individuals), we use a technique called kernel density estimation (KDE): it smooths out the locations of everyone who shares a name into a continuous surface, showing where the name is more or less concentrated, the same way a heatmap works. The coloured contours enclose areas where bearers of the name were most concentrated, measured as where this population-weighted density is highest. See de Smith, Goodchild and Longley (2018) and Cheshire and Longley (2011) for the technique itself, and Van Dijk and Longley (2020) for how we apply it here. Further analysis of what these surname regions show is published in Kandt et al. (2020).

We only build a map, or show any statistic, for a name with at least 100 people recorded in a given year - never fewer. This is to make sure nobody's individual circumstances can be inferred from the page, and it's also why a search for a very rare name may come back with no results at all. For the 1911 and 1921 censuses, Scotland has no data of its own; rather than show a name's map with a blank hole where Scotland should be, a name that is strongly concentrated in Scotland instead shows its 1901 map for those two years, with a note explaining the substitution.

Understanding your results

Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD)

Neighbourhood deprivation is often used to understand behaviours and other outcomes. GeoDS has standardised IMD indices for England, Scotland and Wales for every SmartCensus year and standardised them to the 1-100 range. This makes it possible to see how many bearers of your name live in neighbourhoods placed in each deprivation decile.

There is no obvious reason why people with common surnames such as 'Smith' should live in more or less deprived neighbourhoods than those with surnames such as 'Baker' - and the SmartCensus confirms this. However, rarer surnames with distinctive geographies often tell a different story - compare 'McFee' with 'Offer' for example. Differences can reflect historical differences in the local economic opportunities where different family names originated. It is astonishing that such inequalities between family groups are apparent in the SmartCensus, collected more than 800 years after surnames came into common parlance. Read more in Longley et al. (2021). In this context, we can also observe that the neighbourhood circumstances of many more recently arrived ethnic minority populations have not yet converged with the longer established population - read more in Longley et al. (2023).

We show the decile (1 = most deprived, 10 = least deprived) that bearers of your name most often live in, and a composite GBNames deprivation score (the average percentile and its spread). England, Wales and Scotland are each ranked on their own and then compared directly - a known simplification.

The UK's Geodemographic Structure (OAC)

The UK Output Area Classification (OAC) is a classification of neighbourhoods, arranged into Supergroups and Groups based upon 2021/22 Census of Population data. We created this through a collaboration with the Office for National Statistics (ONS) - you can find out how in Wyszomierski et al. (2023). We show the distribution of your selected family group across 2021/22 OAC Supergroups and Groups, with links to the attributes (pen portraits) of these neighbourhoods. See the dataset page.

London's Geodemographic Structure (LOAC)

As the UK capital, some attributes of Greater London's distinctive neighbourhoods are not described fully by nationwide geodemographic classifications. The London Output Area Classification (LOAC) therefore uses the same methods used to create the UK Output Area Classification (described in Longley et al. (2024)) but applies them only to Greater London. We show the distribution of your selected family group across 2021 LOAC Supergroups and Groups, with links to the attributes of these neighbourhoods. See the dataset page.

The Geography of Gambling (GB2C)

The Great Britain Gambling Behaviours Classification (GB2C) provides the first national neighbourhood classification of gambling behaviours in Great Britain, based on observed online transactional behaviours drawn from industry data. We show the distribution of your selected family group across gambling subgroups, with links to the characteristics of these behaviours. See the dataset page; the accompanying paper is currently under review.

How Healthy are our Neighbourhoods? (AHAH)

Access to Healthy Assets and Hazards (AHAH) is a classification of the health attributes associated with neighbourhoods. Data used include access to different retail facilities, health services and physical features. The graphic shows the neighbourhood health profile for bearers of your selected surname (1 = healthiest, 10 = least healthy - the opposite direction to IMD). See Green et al. (2018) and the dataset page.

Thriving in or Surviving in Britain's Cost of Living Crisis? (FPC)

Financial 'precarity' describes a state of economic insecurity arising from unpredictable employment and low levels of social protection. Neighbourhood measures of this underpin the GeoDS Financial Precarity Classification, described in Zi and Singleton (2026). Every indicator above shows the most common (modal) group, plus the full spread across every group bearers of your name are found in - click a classification's name to read its official description where one exists.

More about your name

How many?

1851-1921: the number of (adult and child) bearers of your name in our database (1911 counts exclude Scotland).

1997-2026: the estimated number of adult bearers of your name based in the SmartCensus.

Where did they come from and where do they live today?

The Historic Census parishes, and the present-day SmartData areas, your name's bearers are most concentrated in, most common first.

What are the favourite given names?

The most common forenames recorded alongside your surname, by sex, for the Historic Census (1851-1921) and SmartData (1997-2026) periods.

Ethnicity Estimator

Given and family names provide clues as to probable ethnicity. We show a rough estimate of the most common census ethnic group among bearers of the surname you entered - not a record of any individual's own identity. Better estimates can be obtained using the full names classification software available through GeoDS; see Kandt and Longley (2018).

Privacy

GBNames is intended to show interesting geographical patterns in names, rather than mapping individuals or families. We do not map or show statistics for names with fewer than 100 occurrences per year. In total we have statistics for over 40,000 unique surnames; these names account for approximately 85 per cent of the 2026 adult population in England, Wales and Scotland. See the sections above for more on the methodology we use.

GBNames follows the UCL Privacy Policy and the UCL Cookie Policy. Please refer to these pages to understand what data we collect and how we process it.
