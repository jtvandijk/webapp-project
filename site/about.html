About GBNames

GBNames and the SmartCensus are a programme of research by the ESRC Geographic Data Service (GeoDS) into the places where British family groups have lived, moved and changed over the last 175 years. This page explains, in plain terms, where the data comes from, how the map is built, and what each of the statistics on a name's page actually shows - with links to the underlying research for anyone who wants the full detail.

Where the data comes from

For the Historic Census period (1851-1921), we use digitised historical census records for England, Wales and Scotland from the Integrated Census Microdata (I-CeM) project, who integrate the decennial censuses under secure data-user agreements with researchers. Addresses are linked to the historic parish they fall in; see Higgs and Schürer (2014) for how this was built. Census data for a given year only becomes available for research once it is 100 years old, which is why 1921 is currently the most recent census year we can use, and why Scotland is missing from the 1911 census (not released on the same schedule as England and Wales).

For the SmartData and SmartCensus periods (1997 onwards), we use consumer registers: large, anonymised commercial datasets that record adults at their postal address. Details of these data are in Lansley et al. (2019) and Van Dijk et al. (2021). The most recent year, labelled SmartCensus, is the newest wave of this data and the namesake of the whole programme.

How we map your name

Rather than plotting every single address (which would risk identifying individuals), we use a technique called kernel density estimation (KDE): it smooths out the locations of everyone who shares a name into a continuous surface, showing where the name is more or less concentrated, the same way a heatmap works. See de Smith, Goodchild and Longley (2018) and Cheshire and Longley (2011) for the technique itself, and Van Dijk and Longley (2020) for how we apply it here. Further analysis of what these surname regions show is published in Kandt et al. (2020), Longley et al. (2021) and Longley et al. (2023).

We only build a map, or show any statistic, for a name with at least 100 people recorded in a given year - never fewer. This is to make sure nobody's individual circumstances can be inferred from the page, and it's also why a search for a very rare name may come back with no results at all. For the 1911 and 1921 censuses, Scotland has no data of its own; rather than show a name's map with a blank hole where Scotland should be, a name that is strongly concentrated in Scotland instead shows its 1901 map for those two years, with a note explaining the substitution.

Understanding your results

Below the map, a name's page shows a series of indicators - each one a classification of the neighbourhoods where bearers of that name live, plus some further facts about the name itself. They appear in this order:

Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD)

Neighbourhoods across Great Britain can be ranked from most to least deprived, taking into account income, employment, health, education, housing and more. We show the decile (1 = most deprived, 10 = least deprived) that bearers of your name most often live in, and a composite GBNames deprivation score (the average percentile and its spread). England, Wales and Scotland are each ranked separately and then compared directly - a known simplification. See the English, Welsh and Scottish indices for full detail.

UK Output Area Classification (OAC)

A classification of every neighbourhood in the UK into a Supergroup and a more detailed Group, based on 2021/22 census data - things like age, housing, occupation and ethnicity. We show the Supergroup and Group bearers of your name most often live in, and the short "pen portrait" description behind each one. See Wyszomierski et al. (2023) and the dataset page.

London Output Area Classification (LOAC)

The same idea as OAC, but built specifically for Greater London, whose population is not always well described by a nationwide classification. See Singleton and Longley (2015).

GB2C Gambling Classification

A classification of neighbourhoods by online gambling behaviour, built by GeoDS from around 1.2 million anonymised online gambling accounts. We show the group bearers of your name most often live in. See the dataset page for more detail; the accompanying paper is currently under review.

Access to Healthy Assets and Hazards (AHAH)

A multi-dimensional index describing how healthy a neighbourhood is to live in, based on access to things like green space, healthy food, GPs and leisure services, as well as exposure to hazards like air pollution, fast-food outlets and tobacco retailers. We show the decile bearers of your name most often live in (1 = healthiest, 10 = least healthy - the opposite direction to IMD). See Green et al. (2018) and the dataset page.

Financial Precarity Classification (FPC)

A classification of neighbourhoods by financial precarity - how exposed households are to problem debt, insecure work and financial shocks. We show the group bearers of your name most often live in. See Zi and Singleton (2026).

Every indicator above shows the most common (modal) group, plus the full spread across every group bearers of your name are found in - click a classification's name to read its official description where one exists.

More about your name

Places: the parishes (Historic Census) and areas (SmartData) your name's bearers are most concentrated in, most common first.

Forenames: the most common given names recorded alongside your surname, by sex, for the Historic Census and SmartData periods.

Ethnicity Estimator: given and family names can provide clues as to ethnicity. We show the single most common census ethnic group estimated from the forenames and surnames of your name's bearers - a rough estimate, not a record of any individual's own identity. Better estimates are possible with full names; see Kandt and Longley (2018).

Number of Bearers: how many people have shared your name in each year we have data for. SmartData and SmartCensus figures are adjusted so that the register itself growing or shrinking over time doesn't look like your name becoming more or less common - see the map's own period slider for the same figures year by year.

Privacy

GBNames is intended to show interesting geographical patterns in names, not to map individuals or families. We never map or show statistics for a name with fewer than 100 people recorded in a given year.

GBNames follows the UCL Privacy Policy and the UCL Cookie Policy. Please refer to these pages to understand what data we collect and how we process it.
