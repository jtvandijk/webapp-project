Welcome to GBNames and the SmartCensus

The SmartCensus is a programme of research by the Geographic Data Service (GeoDS) to explore the places where we live, work and play. It uses geography to link new ‘smart’ data from our digital devices to conventional statistics, so that we can trace population movements across the generations. The SmartCensus can chart inter-generational movements of your family group across Great Britain over the last 175 years, while also documenting changes in its estimated size. Where our ancestors lived can also shape where we live today, so do also compare the neighbourhoods where your family group is concentrated with the population at large. While geography is not always destiny, panning and zooming across the map helps you to trace your family's geographic origins, as well as helping to explain its SmartCensus profile.

About GBNames and the SmartCensus

GBNames explores the British geography of family names, using the search box above. Family groups often settled in different parts of Great Britain and spread out over time, so they can end up concentrated in different kinds of neighbourhoods. This site uses surnames to introduce the SmartCensus, developed by the Economic and Social Research Council's Geographic Data Service (GeoDS).

Where the data comes from

For the Historic Census period (1851-1921), we use digitised census records from the I-CeM project. Census data only becomes available for research once it is 100 years old, which is why 1921 is currently the most recent year we can use. The SmartCensus links conventional censuses with new 'smart' data sources - updated every year since 1997 - to give more detailed, more frequently updated neighbourhood estimates than conventional statistics allow. See data.geods.ac.uk, Lansley et al. (2019) and Van Dijk et al. (2021) for the detail. We have been updating many of our SmartCensus datasets every year since 1997, allowing researchers to monitor the intricacies of local changes throughout the 21st century. See our About page for the full detail and citations.

How we map the distributions from these data sources

We use a technique called kernel density estimation to show where a name's bearers are most concentrated, without plotting individual addresses. We only build a map, or show a statistic, for a name with at least 100 people in a given year, to protect individual privacy. See our About page for the full technique and citations.
